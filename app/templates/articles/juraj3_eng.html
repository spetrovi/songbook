Saint George in an Interethnic Context

In Bulgaria, on Saint George’s Day, girls weave three wreaths from flowers. To whom were these wreaths intended? (1)

(1) Terzic A, Bjeljac Z, Karadzoski V, Jovanovic R. Saint George’s day in the Balkans - customs and rituals in Bulgaria, Serbia and Macedonia. Journal of the Geographical Institute Jovan Cvijic, SASA. 2018;68(3):383-397. doi:10.2298/ijgi180130003t

In 1942 in Sarajevo, celebrations of Saint George’s Day (Serbian Đurđevdan, Romani Ederlezi) took place on the night of May 5 to May 6 (April 23–24 in the Julian calendar).

These celebrations were interrupted by the Ustaše police, who arrested Sarajevo Serbs, Roma, and Jews and loaded them into transport wagons. One wagon was intended for 7 horses or 40 soldiers, yet according to testimonies, approximately 200 prisoners were loaded into a single wagon.

The prisoners were transported to the Jasenovac concentration camp. According to survivor Žarko Vidović(2), after boarding the wagon, one of the prisoners began singing a St. George’s song with the following lyrics:

“Spring descends upon my shoulders, green lily of the valley, for everyone except me – it is Saint George’s Day!”

“Jar si sadla na moje ramená, konvalinka zelená, pre všetkých okrem mňa – je sviatok sv. Juraja” – free translation

Others gradually joined in the singing, which provoked the Ustaše to impose even harsher repression. Of approximately 3,000 deported prisoners, only about 2,000 are believed to have survived the journey.

The total number of victims in the Jasenovac concentration camp is estimated at between 500,000 and 700,000.

This song was also arranged by Goran Bregović for Emir Kusturica’s film Time of the Gypsies (1988). The Romani version, “Ederlezi,” has become a symbolic remembrance of these events.

Intercultural Contexts

Saint George in an Interethnic Context

The cult of Saint George across Europe and its parallels in different cultures

The cult of Saint George is not a local phenomenon of Central Europe. In Western countries, it is more closely associated with the legend of the dragon slaying, and Saint George is venerated here as a patron of warriors, knights, and soldiers. In his honor, equestrian competitions are held (Austria, Bavaria), and saber dances are performed (Piedmont) (1,2).

In Eastern and Central Europe, however, Saint George is perceived primarily as a protector of vegetation.

Green George and Spring Processions

In the northwestern part of Croatia, some areas of Slovenia, Austria, and Bavaria (1,3,4), processions with Green George took place on April 23–24 — a figure of a young man decorated with branches or ears of grain.

These processions are accompanied by songs about Green George, who brought spring. A similar figure, “Jack in the Green,” is part of May Day celebrations in England.

Parallels with the Myth of Jarilo

Processions with Green George closely resemble spring celebrations dedicated to the fertility deity Jarilo among the Eastern Slavs. In Russia, Belarus, and Ukraine (1,5), a spring festival is held in his honor.

During the celebration, Jarilo was represented by a straw effigy, a young man, or a girl on horseback, wearing a wreath on the head and holding a bundle of grain or green branches.

“Where the figure representing Jarilo steps, there will be a good harvest; where it looks, everything will bloom.”

Hıdırellez and Islamic Traditions

Spring celebrations in this period are also observed among Muslims as the festival of Hıdırellez (Turkey, the Balkans). Muslims identify Saint George with the prophet Elijah (7).

The festival takes place on May 5–6 (April 23–24 in the Julian calendar) and includes ritual bonfires, jumping over them, feasting, and decorating with flowers and wreaths.

An interesting parallel is the decoration of small trees with colorful ribbons, representing people’s wishes. This custom resembles letečko, May trees, or maypoles in Central European tradition.

In 2017, the Hıdırellez festival was inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity (8).

Calendar and Symbolic Connections

The Anatolian calendar used in ancient Turkey divided the year into a cold period (starting on October 28) and a divine period (starting on April 24).

This division corresponds to the agricultural year in our tradition, where the summer season began with the release of livestock to pasture on April 24 (Saint George’s Day) and ended with the final grazing on Demeter Day on October 28.

These dates are connected to the visibility of the Pleiades constellation, which can only be seen during the winter period. In Greek mythology, Demeter was the goddess of earth and agriculture, and her daughter Persephone spent part of the year in the underworld — precisely during the period when the Pleiades are visible (7).

Across the continent, striking parallels emerge, showing how different cultures similarly perceive natural cycles, despite their geographical and historical differences.
