On Saint George’s Day

In many St. George songs, the phrase “the earth opens” appears. Which of the following beliefs did people associate with Saint George and the awakening of the earth?

In Europe, the lighting of fires within annual folk traditions is mainly associated with the summer solstice; however, ritual fires were also important on Saint George’s Day.

In Rybany, in the Topoľčany district, St. George’s fires were banned in 1970. Locals had been lighting such large fires that they were visible from the neighboring village of Chynorany, from where firefighters were dispatched under the assumption that it was an actual fire.

Although the misunderstanding was quickly clarified, fires have not been allowed to be lit on Saint George’s Day in Rybany since then (4).

Folk traditions

On Saint George’s Day

Spring customs, protective rituals, and the cult of Saint George across Europe

Saint George’s Day (April 24) historically marked the beginning of spring, i.e. the start of the agricultural year (1,2). The cult of Saint George spread across Europe from Italy, where it was especially strong in areas under Venetian rule. His relics are still preserved there today (3).

This region preserved the most legends about George as a hero who killed a dragon, but also had the miraculous ability to revive animals (1). The figure of Saint George gradually became associated with spring, vegetation, and the protection of livestock.

Spring beliefs and folk tradition

“Before George, nothing grows; after George, everything grows…” (2)

This period was associated with ideas of nature awakening and entering active growth. Women collected plants with yellow flowers and added them to livestock feed to ensure protection and productivity. Plants such as periwinkle or nettle were believed to protect against evil forces (2).

Rituals and magical practices

Wreath weaving was a common custom. Girls threw wreaths onto trees or into water to symbolically predict the future (2).

Morning dew was collected as a magical means of prosperity and fertility (2).

In the Bošácka valley, after St. George’s fires, girls would roll in the fields to ensure a rich rye harvest (8).

Protection of animals and households

On Saint George’s Day, livestock was taken out to pasture for the first time in the year. Before that, barns were fumigated with herbs or sacred smoke to ensure protection and health (4–7).

Various herbs (wormwood, thyme, rosehip…), incense, or coals from Christmas Eve were used. This fumigation had a protective function and its widespread presence suggests an ancient origin.
